Three things follow from those figures. First, with income so dependent on public contracts and grants, a delay, reduction or retender could quickly affect cash and delivery, so contract-level reporting and cash-flow forecasting matter a great deal. Second, with very little voluntary income to fall back on, financial resilience has to come from sound planning, full cost recovery and sensible reserves. Third, the first job for any new Finance Manager would be to understand whether the 2024/25 deficit was planned, timing-related or structural, and what the audited balance sheet says about reserves and cash cover.

I wouldn't describe this as a turnaround. It's the profile of a well-established, contract-funded charity that needs dependable management information and a trusted adviser at the table, which is exactly what you described today.

Relevant search experience

A similar search I recently completed, and what it means for yours

Case study: Finance Manager, Carers Support Bristol & South Gloucestershire

The brief. Carers Support Bristol & South Gloucestershire is a c.£1.23m-income charity supporting adult and young carers across the two areas, with around 50 staff and roughly three-quarters of its income coming from two local authority contracts. It needed a part-time (three-day) Finance Manager to take ownership of financial control and restricted-fund management, cash flow and investments, budgeting and full cost recovery, board-level financial advice to the CEO and Treasurer, and line management of a Finance Officer. It was a senior, trusted role for an organisation that had just reported a deficit year and needed confidence restored at board level.

The process. The search moved from an initial market map of 93 potential candidates down to 11 taken to detailed CV screening, 6 to first interview, and a final shortlist of 3. Every candidate was assessed against a single, objective framework: ten weighted competency dimensions covering financial control, cash flow and treasury, board advisory, budgeting and full cost recovery, payroll and line management, systems, compliance, autonomy, values and practical fit, agreed with the CEO before interviews began. The final stage combined a competency-based interview with a live presentation exercise built from the charity's own real, audited year-end accounts, in which candidates analysed the actual financial position and presented it to a mock board. Throughout, progress was reported to the CEO and Treasurer weekly, and every finalist received an individually prepared briefing.

What stood out. The final field was exceptionally close, with two candidates tied at the top of the scoring framework going into the final interview. Because the presentation exercise used the charity's real accounts rather than a generic case study, it became the true differentiator. The search also stayed open to the market right up to the final stage: a strong late application was identified and assessed just days before the final interviews, after an earlier shortlisted candidate withdrew. A Finance Manager was appointed.

Why an advert alone has limits

Only around 30% of the market is looking at any one time

At any given moment, roughly 30% of potential candidates are actively searching: reading job boards, setting up alerts and applying. Your advertisement will reach them, and some will be excellent.

30%Actively looking
Visible on job boards. This is who an advert reaches.
70%Not looking, but open to the right opportunity
In post, performing well and rarely on job boards. This is where much of the real quality lies.

The other 70% are the people who are doing well where they are. They're running management accounts, presenting to boards and keeping charities financially sound, so they have no reason to browse vacancies. In a locality like Somerset, where specialist finance skills are hard to find, they'll only hear about Thrive if someone they can trust approaches them directly, explains the opportunity honestly and makes a considered case for a conversation. In my experience, around 95% of the people I place are headhunted.

That's the gap my approach is designed to fill. I keep an advertisement on CharityJob working, and then search the 70% directly, building a talent pool of around 250 people, so the shortlist reflects the whole market rather than only the part of it that happens to be looking this month.

The search challenge

Technical strength, board-level judgement and the right fit

You asked for someone qualified and capable who will act as a critical friend, and the pack describes a role that blends hands-on technical delivery with strategic insight. A good CV isn't enough on its own. The challenge is finding people who have demonstrably done the job, can show it with real numbers, and want to do it in your environment. In practice I'd look across five pools:

  • Charity Finance Managers in contract-funded organisations — strong on restricted funds, full cost recovery and funder reporting; test strategic and board-level advice
  • Finance leaders in health, social care and community organisations — used to public-sector commissioners; test the move to a smaller charity team
  • Senior part-time and portfolio accountants — including those with their own practices who want a meaningful three-day role; test commitment, availability and depth of charity experience
  • Experienced deputies ready to step up — strong on systems and detail; test confidence advising trustees
  • Finance professionals from the public sector and housing — strong on governance and compliance; test fit with Thrive's culture and community focus

Qualification matters too. I'd search for candidates who hold ACCA, CIMA, CIPFA or an equivalent professional qualification as a minimum, and I'd confirm the detail at the briefing. I'd also make sure the practical tests go beyond what a panel interview can reveal, because a polished interview and the ability to deliver are not always the same thing.
