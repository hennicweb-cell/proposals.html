The Royal Osteoporosis Society leads the effort to improve bone health and defeat osteoporosis, with a vision of "No more broken bones. No more broken lives." Its Income & Engagement Strategy sets out an ambition to grow audiences, strengthen supporter engagement, increase membership and fundraising income, and develop more integrated supporter journeys that inspire long-term support and advocacy.

The most recent accounts, for the year ended 31 December 2025, show strong growth, and they also show what the next phase needs to build on. Total income rose from £5.28m to £6.59m, driven primarily by higher legacy receipts, including one exceptional gift, with legacies accounting for around 65% of income. The trustees have identified reducing reliance on legacy income as central to diversification, and the charity recruited more than 4,500 new members and 1,000 new supporters through campaigns in 2025. Alongside that, a CRM re-implementation, a new website provider and a brand refresh were reported ahead of the 40th anniversary in 2026.

Why an advert alone has limits

Only around 30% of the market is looking at any one time

At any given moment, roughly 30% of potential candidates are actively searching: reading job boards, setting up alerts and applying. Any advertisement will reach them, and some will be excellent.

30%Actively looking
Visible on job boards. This is who an advert reaches.
70%Not looking, but open to the right opportunity
In post, performing well and rarely on job boards. This is where much of the real quality lies.

The other 70% are the people who are doing well where they are. They're leading individual giving programmes, delivering results and building teams, so they have no reason to browse vacancies. They'll only hear about the Royal Osteoporosis Society if someone they can trust approaches them directly, explains the opportunity honestly and makes a considered case for a conversation.

That's the gap my approach is designed to fill, whenever it might be useful to you. I keep an advertisement on CharityJob working, and then search the 70% directly, so the shortlist reflects the whole market rather than only the part of it that happens to be looking this month.

The search challenge

Deep Individual Giving expertise, with the range to lead a whole function

The specification asks for substantial Individual Giving depth and the leadership to run membership, legacy marketing, community fundraising, events and supporter care. Candidates who are strong in one often need testing on the other, and it's important not to insist on first-hand experience of every income stream, or to let health-charity experience or Dynamics knowledge outweigh stronger evidence of sustainable growth. In practice I'd look across four pools:

  • Heads of Individual Giving ready for a broader remit — strong on acquisition, regular giving and economics; test leadership across membership, legacy and community
  • Existing Heads of Public or Supporter Fundraising — strong on portfolio leadership and direct marketing; test appetite for the scale and change at the Society
  • Senior Individual Giving and membership leaders — strong on teams and budgets; test strategic influence with senior leaders and trustees
  • Fundraising leaders at health and other supporter-led charities — strong on comparable acquisition, retention and legacy programmes; test commercial rigour and results

I'd also distinguish carefully between what someone personally led, the position they inherited, the investment they had, and the results over what period. That separates sustainable growth from exceptional receipts, inherited programmes, or increases bought with substantially higher spend, and it matters particularly where legacy income is such a large share of the picture.

How I'd approach this search

A transparent, five-stage process

You would see how the search is progressing at every stage, and you'd have a documented, defensible process to show your colleagues and trustees.

Foundation and briefing

A joint briefing that goes beyond the job description. Together we'd agree what success looks like at 6, 12 and 24 months, how the fundraising review's recommendations translate into investment, structure and objectives for the role, the essential experience and where you're open to transferable backgrounds, and how the post works with colleagues across the Income & Engagement team. We'd also agree the competencies I'll use to assess every candidate.

Talent mapping and outreach

A structured mapping of the four pools, concentrating on the 70% who aren't on job boards. Each person is approached directly with an honest account of the Society, the strategy and the role. Any advertisement continues alongside, and anyone who responds to it is assessed against the same framework.

Weekly transparency

We'd meet weekly throughout. You'd see the talent pool as it's built: who has been identified, who has been approached, who has shown interest, and who has been ruled out and on what basis. Nothing happens behind closed doors, and you can challenge or redirect the search at any point.

Candidate assessment

I interview every candidate I progress against five areas: Individual Giving expertise, financial judgement, supporter experience, people and change leadership, and strategic influence. You receive a written report on each. For finalists I'd suggest a short fictional case study, not a free strategy for the Society: reviewing a simplified public fundraising portfolio, deciding where to test, invest, improve or stop, and explaining payback, dependencies and how they'd bring the team and senior colleagues with them.

Shortlist, selection & offer

A shortlist of three to five candidates who are appointable on both capability and fit, with salary expectations and notice periods. I then support interview design, candidate preparation, references and offer management, and stay in touch with the successful candidate before they start.
